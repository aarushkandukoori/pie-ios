Overview

Pie (SOSP '25) is a programmable serving system: instead of a fixed generation loop, applications ship small WebAssembly programs — inferlets — that program the serving loop itself: branch and rewind the KV cache, persist it under a name, apply custom sampling. This project ports Pie to the place where programmable serving may matter most: a phone in someone's pocket, where the data is personal and the network is optional.

The result so far is Pie Voice, an iOS app you hold a spoken conversation with. As of September 21, 2026 it runs on a physical iPhone 16 Pro; the measured numbers are in the benchmarks. Speech is transcribed by the OS recognizer (on-device on real hardware — the app's header badge reports the actual mode), answered by Qwen3-0.6B running through the Pie engine embedded inside the app, and spoken back. iOS forbids JIT compilation, so inferlets execute under wasmtime's Pulley interpreter — permissible because Pulley generates no code at runtime (no writable-executable memory). The interpretation overhead is acceptable because inferlets are control-plane code; the FLOPs live in the native driver.

What makes it a Pie app rather than a chat wrapper: the conversation's KV state lives inside the engine under a named snapshot, carried across turns by the voice-chat inferlet. A follow-up question prefills only the new utterance — not the whole transcript. The numbers are in the benchmarks, and you can watch the cost not being paid in the app walkthrough below.

On Your iPhone

Runs on hardware as of September 21, 2026: on an iPhone 16 Pro (iOS 26.1) the embedded engine boots, loads Qwen3-0.6B, and answers a scripted three-turn conversation on the device (numbers in the benchmarks); the spoken path runs on the phone the same way it does in the walkthrough above, with the header badge reporting whether recognition is on-device. Two ways to run it on a physical iPhone (iOS 17+) today. Not on the App Store yet; a TestFlight beta needs an Apple Developer Program membership and will be linked here once one exists.

Build from source (recommended). Clone the ios-voice branch and, with your iPhone plugged in and unlocked, run bash ios/PieVoice/deploy-device.sh from the repository root. It builds the engine and the inferlet, signs with your team (a free Apple ID works), installs, launches, and reads the phone's own log back to confirm the engine booted. Details in ios/PieVoice/README.md.

Sideload the prebuilt build. An unsigned PieVoice.ipa (405 MB, release v0.3.0) installs via AltStore or Sideloadly, which re-sign it with your own Apple ID. Free accounts refresh every 7 days.

What the phone taught us: the same engine defaults that ran fine in the Simulator hit two fatal limits on real hardware, one after the other: wasmtime's pool reservation aborted the process during engine boot (first seen August 26), and once that was sized down, ggml's scheduler context aborted the first forward pass (September 21). The reason is in the benchmarks: the kernel grants an iPhone process only about 5.2 GiB in a single reservation, and both defaults were sized for a datacenter.

What broke on the phone and not in the Simulator: a macOS process (the Simulator is one) has terabytes of virtual address space, so oversized reservations cost nothing there; an iPhone process is capped at about 5.2 GiB per reservation, so two engine defaults sized for servers failed outright. wasmtime's pooling allocator asked for about 3.9 TiB of address space (1000 slots × 4 GiB) and the engine aborted during boot; after that, ggml's backend scheduler asked for about 10.8 GiB of split-input context for a 524,288-node graph budget and aborted in the first forward pass. iPhone builds now use a 4 × 128 MiB wasm pool and a 16,384-node budget. Under the same 5.2 GiB ceiling the 1.7B rung is expected to fit; 4B and 8B are not expected to map without the entitlement, because the driver keeps the GGUF mmap and also copies the weights into buffers. Those rungs are not yet measured on the phone.

Prefill/reuse token counts are exact and deterministic — they are the engine's own accounting, and they are the point: follow-up prefill stays flat as the conversation grows.

Architecture

The app is three layers behind three protocols, and the Pie-facing layer is the only one that knows Pie exists. Everything version-specific — engine config, model path, inferlet id — lives in one file (PieRuntimeConfig.swift), so upgrading to a new Pie release is a diff to that file plus a rebuild of the C shim.

The turn loop is the Pie thesis in miniature: which tokens to prefill, where the conversation state lives, and what is allowed to reach the speaker are all decisions made by a replaceable user-level program — not by the engine.
